# How blind and low-vision readers in Bangladesh (and nearby) read print today, the alternatives, and who pays for assistive technology

Research date: 27 November 2026. Method: public web pages and web search only. Format of each finding: fact, then source (publisher, publication date, URL). "Date not shown" means the page carries no date. "Snippet only" means I saw the claim in a search result but did not open the page, so treat it as unconfirmed. Currency notes: Tk = Bangladeshi taka, Rs/INR = Indian rupee.

## 1. Phone apps: price, printed Bangla (Bengali) reading, Bangla speech, platform, offline use, limits

### Takeaway
Of the mainstream free camera apps, only Google Lookout (Android only) and the Envision AI app officially list Bengali. Microsoft Seeing AI, Supersense and the Be My Eyes app do not list Bengali. All of these apps are free or cheap, so price is not the barrier. The barriers are Bangla coverage, Bangla OCR and voice quality, whether they work offline, and the blind user's need to aim a phone at a page. Sighted help is still needed when the aim is off.

### Cited Findings
**Google Lookout (Android)**
- Lookout's official language list has 35 languages, including Bengali, Hindi, Gujarati, Kannada, Marathi, Tamil and Telugu. Urdu, Nepali and Sinhala are not listed. In Text, Documents and Explore modes you can change the language (default "Detect language"). Image descriptions are English-only. Currency mode covers only USD, EUR and Indian rupees, so there is no Bangladeshi taka. Requires Android 6.0 or later. Google Accessibility Help, "Lookout supported languages" (no article date, page footer ©2026): https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/9031274
- The same help page mentions offline use only for Food labels mode, after a data download. It does not say whether Text or Documents mode works offline. Same source.
- Lookout is free (Google Play app, no price listed on the help page). Same source. No iOS version is mentioned.

**Microsoft Seeing AI (iOS and Android)**
- Free. Seeing AI official site (no date shown): https://www.seeingai.com/
- The App Store "Languages" field lists English plus 18 others: Czech, Danish, Dutch, Finnish, French, German, Greek, Hungarian, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Norwegian Bokmål, Polish, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Swedish, Turkish. Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Nepali, Tamil and Sinhala are not listed. Version 5.6.1, released 4 Sep 2025. The Read tools give audio cues for capturing a printed page, and version 5.5 added PDF recognition. Apple App Store listing (viewed Nov 2026): https://apps.apple.com/us/app/seeing-ai/id999062298
- At the Android launch Seeing AI had 18 languages, and Microsoft planned 36 in 2024. Microsoft Accessibility Blog, Dec 2023: https://blogs.microsoft.com/accessibility/seeing-ai-app-launches-on-android-including-new-and-updated-features-and-new-languages/
- A later report says it reached 33 languages. Windows Report (date not shown, snippet only): https://windowsreport.com/?p=1331883. This conflicts with the App Store's 19 listed languages. The App Store field may count only interface languages.

**Envision AI app (iOS and Android)**
- Free on the App Store. The description says it reads text in "over 60 different languages". The Languages field lists English plus 45 more, and Bengali is on the list. It offers "audio-guided edge detection" for scanning single or multiple pages. Version 3.5.4 (year not shown). Apple App Store (viewed Nov 2026): https://apps.apple.com/us/app/envision-ai/id1268632314
- On Android the language must also be supported by Google TTS for the app to speak results. Envision support page, via search snippet only (the page returned 403 to me): https://support.letsenvision.com/hc/en-us/articles/8118510691217. That page has an online/offline language table, which I could not open. Bengali's offline status is therefore unconfirmed.

**Be My Eyes / Be My AI (iOS and Android)**
- Free. The App Store Languages field has 44 languages, including Hindi and Urdu. Bengali, Nepali, Tamil and Sinhala are not listed. The description says Be My AI (GPT-4-based image description) supports 36 languages. Version 7.21.0 (Nov, year not shown). Apple App Store: https://apps.apple.com/us/app/be-my-eyes/id905177575
- Volunteers speak 185 languages and are available for free 24 hours a day. Be My Eyes App Store / support pages via search snippet: https://support.bemyeyes.com/hc/en-us/articles/360005528557-Getting-Started-with-Be-My-Eyes. A Bengali-speaking volunteer pool is not confirmed.

**Supersense (iOS)**
- $4.99/month, $49.99/year or $99.99 lifetime, plus a $99.99 one-time licence for schools and organisations. Languages: English, Croatian, Danish, Dutch, French, German, Hindi, Hungarian, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Turkish. No Bengali. The description says most features work offline. Apple App Store (via search, date not shown): https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1484547836

**KNFB Reader, now OneStep Reader (iOS)**
- Development moved from the National Federation of the Blind to Sensotec in 2022, and the app was renamed OneStep Reader. Sensotec (date not shown): https://sensotec.be/en/?p=34752
- Price $99.99. Version 3.7.1 is dated 18 Feb 2026, so it is still maintained. Third-party App Store mirror: https://iphone.apkpure.com/knfb-reader/be.sensotec.knfb.reader/amp. I did not check its language list.

**Android TalkBack and Bangla speech or screen readers in Bangladesh**
- Bangla text-to-speech and screen readers "still lag" English in accuracy and fluency and often mispronounce words. Bangla OCR prototypes "mostly remain in labs or pilot projects". A high-quality Bangla TTS exists but government e-services rarely use it. Vashkar Bhattacharjee (a blind accessibility expert), The Business Standard, 27 Nov 2025: https://www.tbsnews.net/node/1296496
- a2i's website offers a download of the free NVDA screen reader for Windows (version 2020.4). a2i (page viewed Nov 2026): https://a2i.gov.bd/?p=4709
- eGeneration's "Alo" Bangla screen reader was shown at a workshop. I found no adoption data. TBS (date not shown): https://www.tbsnews.net/node/320095
- An eSpeak Bengali voice with NVDA was tested in Kolkata in Dec 2014. Participants "showed some reservations regarding its voice". Centre for Internet & Society India (2014–15): https://cis-india.org/accessibility/blog/report-on-use-of-espeak-bengali-with-nvda
- Only 1 of 6 widely used assistive apps tested offered Hindi text recognition. arXiv 2407.17496 (2024), search snippet only (I could not open the PDF): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.17496

**"Speak!" app**
- I could not identify which app this is. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- The only mainstream option with official Bengali is Lookout, and it needs an Android phone. Envision is the second. A blind Bangladeshi student with a mid-range Android phone can already get free Bangla OCR today. The open questions are quality, offline use, and the effort of aiming and capturing a page.
- Seeing AI, Be My Eyes and Supersense are mainly useful in English. That still matters, because SenseBook's English reading competes with free tools.
- Every app needs the user to hold and aim the phone (see Section 6). A fixed clip-on camera is a real difference only if it removes aiming and page-framing work. The product case should rest on that, not on "Bangla OCR exists nowhere".

### Gaps
- Lookout: whether Text or Documents mode reads Bengali offline, and how accurate Bangla OCR is on textbook pages. No published accuracy figure was found.
- Envision: Bengali in offline mode (the support table was blocked) and current paid tiers. The App Store shows "Free" but no in-app prices.
- Be My AI's 36-language list. Not confirmed whether Bengali is among them.
- "Speak!": no clear match found.
- No independent test of Bangla OCR accuracy across apps was found.

## 2. Dedicated devices: prices and Bangla support

### Takeaway
Western dedicated readers (OrCam, Envision Glasses) cost about US$1,900–4,400 and do not confirm Bengali. Indian desk-type readers cost much less. Trestle Labs' Kibo XS lists Bengali OCR but needs a computer and internet. IIT-founder-built URead is an offline flatbed-and-arm reader at Rs 30,000 (2022) but did not confirm Bengali. Ray-Ban Meta glasses with Be My Eyes reached India in 2025, but not Bangladesh. SenseBook's planned Tk 16,990 (~US$138) including 2 years of service would sit below every dedicated device found.

### Inferences
- Price ladder found (prices as listed, not converted by source): free apps < Kibo Desk ~Rs 1,550/yr < Kibo XS Rs 10,000/yr or Rs 27,000 perpetual (subsidised) ≈ URead Rs 30,000 (2022) ≈ Ray-Ban Meta Rs 29,990–39,900 < Envision Glasses $1,899–3,499 < OrCam Read 3 $2,790 < OrCam MyEye 3 Pro £4,399. At about Tk 16,990 / US$138 with 2 years of service, SenseBook would be the cheapest dedicated reader found. It would still be far above free phone apps.
- Kibo is the closest direct competitor. It is Indian, lists Bengali OCR, sells to institutions and blind schools, and has a subsidy model. But it is tied to a computer and the internet, while SenseBook plans a screen-free, clip-on, touch-and-voice device. This is a point to check with users, not a proven advantage.
- Indian CSR already buys AI reading glasses for blind students (IEX, ManpowerGroup), which is a precedent for SenseBook's sponsor model. None of these programmes disclosed a cost per device.

- Kibo XS list price outside subsidy schemes, and any Bangladesh sales or deployments.
- Whether Kibo's Bengali OCR has been tested on NCTB textbooks.
- Ray-Ban Meta or Be My Eyes availability in Bangladesh: not listed, and no official statement found.
- Cost per unit for TorchIT Jyoti AI Pro and the Smart Vision Glasses.

## 3. What Bangladesh already provides: talking textbooks, braille, screen readers, integrated education, NGOs

### Takeaway
Bangladesh already gives visually impaired school students free DAISY multimedia talking textbooks for classes 1–10, produced by YPSA with a2i funding and distributed by NCTB since 2023. It also gives free braille textbooks, but they come late and in short supply. The braille code for science and maths was only standardised in 2024, and its print guidelines were still pending in April 2025. Government special education is small: DSS integrated units at 76 centres in 64 districts, plus 12 special schools. NGOs (YPSA, VIPS, BERDO, Sightsavers and partners) fill gaps with smartphones, braille books and training. Above class 10, almost nothing accessible is provided.

### Cited Findings
**a2i / YPSA / NCTB multimedia talking books (DAISY)**
- YPSA developed DAISY multimedia textbooks for classes 1–10, including maths, plus EPUB 3 e-books and digital braille, with a2i funding (Innovation Grant 2013, Service Innovation Fund 2015). Project period 1 Apr 2013 to 31 Dec 2015. The books are distributed free on DVD and as downloads, and given out each year on 1 January to primary and secondary schools and madrasas. Students play them on DVD players, Android phones or MP3 players. About 150,000 users (self-reported). Used in 5 schools for the blind and 64 integrated schools. Higher secondary and tertiary books are not yet converted. YPSA project page (date not shown): https://ypsa.org/accessible-reading-materials-for-the-students-with-visual-and-print-disabilities
- NCTB announced that from 1 January 2023 all class 1–10 books would be readable through a "Multimedia Talking Book" application and downloadable free from the NCTB website. NCTB was to fund distribution from then on; before that, YPSA supplied the books with a2i technical support. The books were first launched on 31 Dec 2014. BSS, 6 Dec 2022 (Bangla): https://www.bssnews.net/news/98326 ; Business Post BD, Dec 2022: https://businesspostbd.com/news/talking-books-for-visually-impaired-students-from-january-2023
- YPSA (with ABC/WIPO and Australian Government funding) produces braille books, audiobooks, accessible PDFs and e-books, DAISY talking books and large print, and trains students to use screen readers and reading apps. In 2025 ABC delivered 240,500 accessible files worldwide; its partners have made 24,000+ textbooks accessible in 40+ low-income countries. WIPO Accessible Books Consortium, 21 Jul 2026: https://www.wipo.int/en/web/abc/w/news/2026/when-books-became-freedom-how-two-students-found-their-voice
- YPSA also launched an accessible Bangla–English dictionary (web and mobile) in February 2018. YPSA, Feb 2018: https://ypsa.org/2018/02/prime-minister-launched-accessible-dictionary-for-visually-impaired

**Braille textbooks: supply and delays**
- Since 2017 the government has given free braille books for primary and secondary levels, but they cover only part of the need. A braille copy costs about 3 times a normal book ("if a normal book costs Tk100, the Braille version costs three times that"). New standard braille notations for science and maths were approved in Nov 2024, but print guidelines had not been released as of April 2025. The Business Standard, 23 Apr 2025: https://www.tbsnews.net/node/1124151
- The government approved braille guidelines in September 2024 for a single code covering Bengali, English, Maths and Arabic, and printing was "still in progress". BERDO Executive Director at a Prothom Alo roundtable, published 28 Jul 2025: https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/roundtable/loscvcsh4l. The month conflicts with TBS's "November 2024"; the approval may have come in stages.
- The government distributed 9,504 braille textbooks in one year, "low compared to the demand". Prothom Alo English (year not shown): https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/Braille-books-scarce-in-Bangladesh
- In 2016, NCTB planned 9,703 braille textbook copies for 12,100 students from primary to class IX. The Independent, 24 Nov 2016 (older source): https://theindependentbd.com/arcprint/details/69634/2016-11-24
- Producing braille books takes 3–4 months, so students cannot get the current edition at the start of the school year. IFLA paper by V. Bhattacharjee, 2019 (older source): https://library.ifla.org/id/eprint/2733/1/S22-2019-bhattacharjee-en.pdf
- At higher secondary level, only Bashundhara Exercise Book and View Foundation provide braille books. In Dec 2025 Bashundhara gave NCTB-approved braille textbooks to about 50 visually impaired students from 18 colleges. Bangladesh Pratidin English, 15 Dec 2025 (snippet only): https://en.bd-pratidin.com/national/2025/12/15/52833
- Visually impaired students got their textbooks with "half the educational calendar already past". bdnews24 (date not shown, snippet only): https://bdnews24.com/bangladesh/braille-books-yet-to-reach-schools
- Few general books exist in braille, and publishers seldom produce them. VIPS asked for state subsidies for accessible materials at the Ekushey Boimela 2026. The Financial Express, 4 Mar 2026: https://today.thefinancialexpress.com.bd/print/visually-impaired-readers-deprived-as-most-books-lack-braille-editions-1772554320

**Integrated Education Programme (Department of Social Services, DSS)**
- DSS Director General (July 2025): "Educational services for visually impaired students are being conducted at 76 centers across 64 districts." Braille and sign-language teaching is also given in 12 special schools. A DSS deputy director said blind pupils attend nearby mainstream schools and must rely on a stenographer (scribe) in SSC exams. Prothom Alo, 28 Jul 2025: https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/roundtable/loscvcsh4l
- The programme began in 1974 with 47 units and later ran 64 units in selected secondary schools. Each unit gets a resource teacher and a resource room. Banglapedia (date not shown): https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Disability
- An older Daily Star report says there were 30 resource teachers for 64 districts and about 38 of the 64 resource-programme schools were inoperative. The Daily Star (undated "news-detail" URL, likely ~2009, older source): https://www.thedailystar.net/news-detail-72278

**Barriers for students (survey)**
- A VIPS/MJF/GRM study of visually impaired college and university students found: 75.36% cite the high cost of assistive technology, 92.96% lack accessible study materials, 91.55% report a severe shortage of trained teachers, 97% rely on others for online admissions, and about 90% of university websites are inaccessible. Reported in The Business Standard, 23 Apr 2025: https://www.tbsnews.net/node/1124151
- At the same study's launch, participants called for resource centres with assistive technology at each university, removal of customs duty and VAT on imported assistive technology, and free laptops and smartphones from the government. Bangladesh Post (snippet only; page unreachable): https://mail.bangladeshpost.net/posts/barriers-faced-by-students-with-visual-disabilities-likely-to-hamper-achieving-sdgs-140636

**NGOs and schools**
- YPSA gave smartphones to 300 visually impaired students across the country, nearly half of them women, with VIPS offering screen-reader training. YPSA, Apr and May 2018 (older source): https://ypsa.org/2018/04/smart-phone-distribution-among-visually-impaired-women-students ; https://ypsa.org/2018/05/talking-smartphone-distribution-and-orientation-to-visually-challenged-students
- Sightsavers co-led "Inclusive Futures", a consortium of 11 organisations funded with $10 million by UK FCDO, for children aged 4–14. Its delivery partners include the Bangladesh Visually Impaired People's Society and Assistance for Blind Children. FCDO DevTracker: https://devtracker.fcdo.gov.uk/programme/GB-CHC-207544-94101/summary. In 2024 the national inclusive education policy was validated (Oct 2024) and Bengali Braille Guidelines were launched. Sightsavers Asia policy summary 2024 (published Aug 2025): https://www.sightsavers.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Sightsavers-Asia-policy-influencing-annual-summary-2024.pdf
- Baptist Sangha School for Blind Girls started in Mirpur, Dhaka, in 1977 with five blind girls. Since Jan 2007 it has been Baptist Mission Integrated School, teaching blind and sighted children together up to class 8, with braille taught up to grade 2. Bangladesh Baptist Church Sangha (date not shown): https://bbcs.org.bd/institutions/school/baptist-mission-integrated-school,-dhaka.html ; Dhaka Tribune (date not shown): https://www.dhakatribune.com/bangladesh/313706/eyes-of-dreams-visually-impaired-children
- Sparsha Braille Prokashona (run by visually impaired students) and VIPS ran stalls at the 2026 book fair. The Financial Express, 4 Mar 2026 (link above).

### Inferences
- Free DAISY talking books already cover the core NCTB syllabus for classes 1–10. SenseBook will not win on "access to textbooks" for those classes. Its value would be in the books that are not converted: newly revised editions, guidebooks and notes, supplementary readers, library books, higher-secondary and university texts, and exam papers. It would also be in exercises, diagrams and explanation, which audio books do not give.
- Bangladesh changed its curriculum in 2023 and partly reverted it in 2025 (general knowledge, not checked in this research). This makes it likely that some DAISY editions lag the printed ones. This needs checking.
- The integrated programme (76 centres, 64 districts) and 12 special schools are natural pilot sites and channels.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 confirmation that NCTB's talking books were updated for the revised textbooks, or how many students download or use them.
- No official national count of visually impaired students in the integrated programme, or seats per unit. I believe 10 seats per unit is commonly cited, but I did not find a source, so it is unconfirmed.
- No current data on BRAC, CBM, Access Bangladesh Foundation, or a2i's present disability innovation work; the searches found nothing specific.
- Braille textbook copies distributed in 2025 and 2026 (official NCTB figure) not found.

## 4. Size of the population: blindness, low vision, children, age profile

### Takeaway
About 1% of Bangladeshis aged 30+ are blind (Nationwide Blindness Survey 2020, CIPRB), and 19% of them have some visual impairment. Blindness is overwhelmingly age-related and cataract-driven. Only 0.2% of under-55s are blind, against 1.8% of those 55 and over (2013 survey). Blind children are estimated at only about 35,000–40,000. So blind students are a small share of all blind people. Most blind people are older adults whose blindness is mainly treatable.

### Cited Findings
- **Nationwide Blindness Survey 2020** (CIPRB, funded by the National Eye Care programme): 18,810 people aged 30+ in all 64 districts, Nov 2020 to Jan 2021. Blindness about 1% (down 35% in two decades). 19% of those aged 30+ have some visual impairment. Cataract-related blindness is about 1%, equal to roughly 534,000 people (compared with 650,000 cases and 1.53% in 1999–2000). More than 80% of blindness is avoidable. 26% of people over 30 use spectacles. The Daily Star, 8 Dec 2021: https://www.thedailystar.net/node/2913066 ; The Business Standard (Dec 2021): https://www.tbsnews.net/bangladesh/blindness-rate-falls-35-20-years-survey-341074
- One report gives 0.7% rather than 1% for the 30+ group. Unresolved; search snippet only.
- **2013 national survey** (published 2022): 6,391 adults aged 40+. Age-adjusted blindness 1.0%, low vision 12.1%. ### Inferences

- Taken together: a student-only market for SenseBook in Bangladesh is likely in the low tens of thousands of children and youths. The larger group is older adults with cataract or low vision, many of them non-literate or treatable. This favours (a) sponsor-funded school deployments in the low thousands, and (b) checking whether low-vision adults are a real second segment.

## 5. Who pays: allowances, stipends, JPUF and DSS, donors, CSR, precedents

### Takeaway
State cash support is small per person: Tk 1,000/month disability allowance proposed for FY2026-27 (3.8 million recipients), and Tk 900–1,400/month student stipends for about 81,000–100,000 students. There is no government programme found that buys reading devices. Donors (ATscale, UNICEF, Sightsavers) work at the policy and system level. Bank CSR is required to put a share into education (30% under the 2022 rule), but the total fell about 21% in H1 2026. Telecom CSR for blind people has gone to smart canes (Robi, 2025), not readers. In India, CSR already buys AI reading glasses for blind students, and the ADIP scheme funds accessible phones and laptops. These are the closest precedents for a sponsor-paid device.

## 6. Usability evidence: phone camera OCR problems and hands-free or mounted reading devices

### Takeaway
Research from 2016–2025 keeps finding the same problem. Blind users struggle to aim a phone camera and frame a whole page. Apps flag bad images but rarely say how to fix them, so users retake photos or call sighted help. Reading succeeds on flat printed pages but fails on curved or partial text. Wearable or hands-free readers (OrCam) improved task completion in small pilots, but only 45% of users rated usability positively in a 100-person study. Evidence from South Asia is thin. An Indian diary study again found poor feedback on camera position and lighting.

### Cited Findings
- In a study of 12 blind participants positioning a phone to capture an OCR-readable document, none of the smartphone OCR apps reviewed (including Prizmo) guaranteed a usable capture. Manduchi group, UC Santa Cruz, eScholarship (date not shown, search snippet): https://escholarship.org/content/qt9p9902m4/qt9p9902m4.pdf?t=nrohld
- Among the assistive OCR apps surveyed, none offered real-time feedback for document capture. eScholarship (date not shown, snippet): https://escholarship.org/content/qt2v52x0m2/qt2v52x0m2.pdf?t=n31yb9
- Getting an OCR-readable image is "very challenging" for some blind users because they cannot see the screen. NIH grant R21-EY025077 (Grantome, older source): https://grantome.com/grant/NIH/R21-EY025077-01
- Neat, Peng, Qin and Manduchi compared Seeing AI's Short Text mode, a vibrate-on-text "Spot+OCR" app and a "Guided OCR" multi-shot app with 7 blind participants. Proceedings of IUI 2019: https://escholarship.org/content/qt50n43705/qt50n43705.pdf
- Be My AI study (14 visually impaired participants: 10 blind, 4 low vision): 8 had trouble aiming the camera. The app flagged images as cut off or blurry but gave no guidance on repositioning ("I didn't know how to put the label on the frame"). Users retook photos, got general descriptions and had to ask follow-ups for specific text. 8 turned to human assistants to fix camera alignment. CHI '25, arXiv 2502.16098, 22 Feb 2025: https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16098v1
- An exploratory 2024 study of 14 users who had used Be My AI for at least 2 months found that they "accommodate its limitations and creatively adapt". arXiv 2407.08882, Jul 2024 (snippet): https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08882v1
- A 2021 study found that Seeing AI and OrCam read 100% of words in printed material like magazines but only 13% on curved surfaces like pill bottles. Search snippet attributing a 2021 study. Related British Library record "Evaluation and Comparison of Artificial Intelligence Vision Aids: OrCam MyEye 1 and Seeing AI" (July 2021): https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100128197849_0x000001. With 7 participants it found no significant difference in task time between OrCam MyEye 1 and Seeing AI. Same record.
- OrCam MyEye pilot (UC Davis, JAMA Ophthalmology 2016): 12 legally blind participants scored 2.5/10 on tasks without the device, 9.5 on first use and 9.8 after a week. The device was supplied by OrCam. Medical Xpress, Oct 2016 (older source): https://medicalxpress.com/news/2016-10-wearable-artificial-vision-device-legally.pdf. Critics noted poor low-light performance. IEEE Spectrum (2016): https://spectrum.ieee.org/israeli-startups-vision-device-can-help-nearlyblind-read-and-recognize-faces-study-shows
- Multicentre study in Italy (100 visually impaired people, 5 rehabilitation centres): the OrCam device improved reading and face recognition, but only 45% gave a positive usability rating, and age and visual field defect explained most of the variation. Research Square preprint (~2022, not peer-reviewed): https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-1824900/latest.pdf
- India: a 7-day diary study of smartphone object-recognition apps with blind and low-vision people found a major difficulty in the lack of accessible feedback on camera positioning, lighting and output quality. CHI '25, Swansea University and Indian collaborators, doi 10.1145/3706598.3713107 (snippet only; repository unreachable): https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa68876
- Indian-language scene-text recognition still makes errors in script identification, detection and characters, including cross-script confusion. Bharat Scene Text benchmark, arXiv 2511.23071, Nov 2025 (snippet): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.23071
- Bangladesh: Bangla screen readers mispronounce academic terms, and Bangla OCR remains mostly in labs or pilots. The Business Standard, 27 Nov 2025: https://www.tbsnews.net/node/1296496

### Inferences
- The evidence supports SenseBook's core idea: a fixed camera that frames the page, so the user does not aim. Aiming and framing are the most repeated failure in phone OCR, across studies from 2016 to 2025.
- The OrCam results warn that even hands-free devices get mixed usability ratings (45% positive). Low light is a known problem. Field tests should measure page-turn flow, lighting and voice clarity, not only OCR accuracy.
- No study measured Bangla OCR accuracy or blind Bangladeshi users' reading tasks with camera apps. SenseBook's own field tests would be the first evidence; they must be reported as untested until done.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on Bangladeshi (or Bengali-reading Indian) blind users with Lookout, Envision or Kibo.
- No study on clip-on or book-mounted cameras for blind readers specifically. The closest are OrCam (wearable) and stand-based scanners (Kibo, URead), with no published evaluations found.
- The 2021 "100% vs 13%" result was seen only in a search snippet. The full paper was not opened.
- Several Manduchi-group and India CHI '25 findings come from search snippets because the PDFs were not readable.
